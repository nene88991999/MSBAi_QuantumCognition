Course Description

A manager may receive different answers to the same question when its wording, position, or preceding discussion changes. The decision is whether to redesign that process, improve a conventional model, or use a model that represents how judgments develop during questioning. This course compares these explanations. A classical probability model assigns probabilities to specified events; a fixed-preference version assumes that asking a question reveals an answer without changing it. Quantum probability provides another mathematical representation in which the sequence of judgments can change the predicted answer. Students examine the restrictions of each model and compare their predictions with evidence.

The applications concern surveys, recommendation systems, and human review of AI advice. A system can influence a decision through the questions and intermediate judgments it requests. The course then examines free will and agency through reasons, reconsideration, and control over the decision procedure. A primer introduces ontology, the study of what exists, and conceptual engineering, the deliberate clarification of a concept for a particular purpose. Students distinguish a model of choice from stronger claims about people. No physics or philosophy background is required. Guided examples use arithmetic, and Python notebooks provide the calculations.

Team Project: A Synthetic Panel, Audited

The project runs in two team-based parts, with a separate individual oral defense. Use the team assigned in Canvas to audit a real, publicly documented company decision. Submit one proposal, report, deck, executed notebook, and set of supporting records per team. Record member contributions and have another member check substantive work. Every member should understand the full audit. The evidence concerns the tested model, prompts, and persona grid. Any proposal to replace human respondents requires separate evidence from the relevant human population.

Part 1. Team research proposal (due end of Week 3). Identify the company decision, two competing frames, and questions A, B, and F. Use public evidence to justify a plausible rival and a fair comparison. Specify the proposed sampling, coding, model settings, and decision thresholds. Pilot the implementation and freeze the collection plan in Week 4. State the action after a clear, practically small, or inconclusive result. See M2A2_Project_Part_1.

Part 2. Team audit report and deck with individual oral defense (Week 8). Collect under the frozen six-condition plan in Weeks 5–6. Analyze order, QQ, and direct-versus-assessed differences with appropriate intervals. Use Week 7 peer critique to improve the report and figures. Assess rivals and the evidence needed for agency or welfare claims. Submit the report, executed notebook, records, 6–10 slides, and attribution, contribution, and revision logs. Present for ten minutes per team; each member then has a separately scored five-minute defense. See M4A1_Project_Part_2.

AI Policy

Each assessment declares a level on the MSBAi AI Assessment Scale: 0 no AI, 1 brainstorming only, 2 drafting with human revision, 3 collaboration with disclosure, and 4 AI as the subject of analysis. Project Part 2 is Level 4, with permitted assistance and disclosure specified in its instructions. The oral defense is Level 0. The reflection is Level 1: write the submitted reflection yourself.

Each team submission includes an AI Attribution Log recording the tools and model versions, prompts, outputs used, member responsible, checks, and revisions. Retain each member’s initial reasoning before consulting AI and verify company facts against public sources. Record contributions and cross-checks in the shared contribution record.

Materials, Tools, and Course Policies

No textbook purchase is required. The repository provides module notes adapted from Quantum Cognition for Management (Yang, manuscript), a philosophy primer, lecture transcripts, and a reading guide with links to articles and author manuscripts. Modules 7–8 revisit these resources while students analyze and explain their evidence. Begin with the suggested introductory readings. Technical and extension readings are optional unless Canvas assigns them.

Use the supplied Lumen collection notebook in VS Code with the instructor-confirmed model and settings. The offline examples and practice audit make no model calls; the collector makes calls only when explicitly enabled. Follow the collection handout for access, logs, costs, and the Week 4 freeze. Use the approved private team repository for project work and submit through Canvas with the repository link and commit identifier. The public course repository supplies readings, templates, and code. Keep credentials and raw interviews in approved private locations. Canvas supplies exact dates and submission links.

Regular live-session and studio attendance is recommended and is not graded separately. The scheduled oral defense is assessed individually. Discussions, Module 3 practice, the oral defense, and the reflection are individual; the proposal, report, deck, and project records are team submissions. The table applies to the entire eight-module course: team work contributes 55% and individual work 45%. Modules 5–8 extend the same project and add no separately graded assignment. Assessment IDs and established filenames are retained for continuity; their numbers do not override the due weeks stated here.
