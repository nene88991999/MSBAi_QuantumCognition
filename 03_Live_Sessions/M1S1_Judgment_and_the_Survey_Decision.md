## Slide 2: The Detection Assumption



What does a detection explanation assume?

What might the act of answering change?

Write one example before discussing it.



### Discussion and Work

Work individually for two minutes, then compare with a partner. Identify the assumption in the example, rather than simply labeling a result a bias.



## Slide 3: The Same Destination, Different Experiences



Which experience comes first may affect the later choice.



Image description: An analyst studies two garden paths that reach the same pavilion. The paths pass a merchant stall and a quiet garden in opposite orders. The illustration introduces a sequence comparison; it reports no data.



### Discussion and Work

The two illustrated paths reach the same pavilion after encountering a market and a garden in different orders. Treat this as an analogy for sequence, not evidence about human behavior. Translate it into two survey procedures.



## Slide 4: A Fictional Service Launch



A company must choose how to introduce a data-enabled service.

One survey leads with convenience; another leads with privacy.

The focal outcome is support for launch.



### Discussion and Work

This is a fictional planning case with no reported results. Hold facts fixed. Ask what the manager wants to decide and which customer outcome would be useful. Define data-enabled service in the discussion as a service that uses customer information to provide a benefit.



## Slide 5: A Fair Comparison



Write the same two questions for both orders.

Keep the same facts and response options.

Specify who answers and how order is assigned.



### Discussion and Work

In pairs, draft ABF and BAF procedures. A is a convenience consideration, B is a privacy consideration, and F is the launch-support decision. Avoid changing the information while claiming to change only order. Save the wording as workshop notes for the proposal.



## Slide 6: A Fixed-Rate Benchmark



Two assessed action rates are 0.30 and 0.70.

Can any weighted average reach 0.80?

State the assumption that this comparison tests.



Imposed conditional rates: 0.30 and 0.70; weights range from 0 to 1.



| Weight on the 0.70 rate | Predicted rate |

| --- | ---: |

| 0 | 0.3 |

| 0.25 | 0.4 |

| 0.50 | 0.5 |

| 0.75 | 0.6 |

| 1.00 | 0.7 |



### Discussion and Work

Use the displayed line or the Module 3 notebook mixture helper. The values are imposed illustrations, not survey findings. A weighted average cannot exceed both fixed inputs. Applying this benchmark to another procedure requires carrying the conditional rates across procedures. Consider how a process-dependent alternative could change them.



## Slide 7: A Test of the Alternative



Could attention, memory, or learning explain the difference?

What does that explanation predict?

Which new comparison would distinguish it?



### Discussion and Work

Each group names one specific alternative and an observable implication. A classical process can depend on context. Avoid using classical as a synonym for an unchanging answer. Connect the discussion to the strongest-rival requirement in Project Part 1.



## Slide 8: A Prediction Before the Result



Choose a new sequence or held-out sample.

Record what each model predicts.

Specify a result that would change your view.



### Discussion and Work

Held-out means kept separate during model fitting or selection. Groups should make a directional or quantitative prediction justified by their design. Do not invent a claim about which model must win.
