# M1M1L3_From a Catalog of Biases to One Framework

In the last lecture we saw three patterns that the classical model struggles with. Today I want to ask how we should organize patterns like these, and I'll start with a story.

At Northwind, a company we'll use as an example, the decision-science team keeps a judgment guide. It lists forty-one recurring judgment patterns, each with an owner, a rule for spotting it, and a local fix.

It's useful: when a familiar problem shows up, someone can look it up. But every time a new pattern appears, someone has to add a new entry.

And the document doesn't help much with the most important question, which is whether a known problem will show up in a decision nobody has seen before. That's where a theory could help.

A good theory would connect several of these patterns and predict when each one should appear or disappear. And because it makes predictions, it would give the team a way to check whether it's right.

Let's look at two entries from the judgment guide, starting with one called the conjunction fallacy. A conjunction just means two things are both true at the same time.

The classic example goes like this. People read a short description of a woman named Linda: she's 31, single, outspoken, and as a student she cared deeply about discrimination and social justice.

Then they're asked which is more likely: that Linda is a bank teller, or that she's a bank teller and active in the feminist movement. Many people pick the second option.

But think about it for a moment. Every feminist bank teller is also a bank teller, so the combination can never be more likely than bank teller on its own.

One explanation is representativeness: people judge how well the description fits a familiar type of person, instead of judging the actual probability. That explains this particular mistake.

A broader theory should also connect it to other patterns.

The second entry is called the disjunction effect. Imagine you've just played a gamble where you had an even chance of winning or losing, and now you're offered the same gamble again.

In a well-known study by the psychologists Amos Tversky and Eldar Shafir, about 69 percent of people who knew they'd won the first round chose to play again. Among people who knew they'd lost, about 59 percent also chose to play.

So in both cases, most people wanted to play. But when people didn't yet know how the first round had gone, only about 36 percent played.

Think about what we'd expect. If the willingness to play after each known outcome carries over unchanged when the outcome is unknown, the unknown rate should be a mix of the two situations.

Under that assumption, it should fall somewhere between 59 and 69. Instead, it fell below both.

The judgment guide would usually file this as a separate problem from the conjunction fallacy, with its own explanation, such as discomfort with not knowing.

So we have two entries that look unrelated. Quantum probability suggests a way they might be connected.

Think back to the arrow picture. Knowing how the first round went snaps the judgment into place, either won or lost.

Not knowing leaves it unsnapped. And as we saw, the unsnapped path can come out higher or lower than the average of the snapped ones.

In this case it comes out lower, so we say the interference term is negative. In other situations, like the fraud example from the last lecture, it pushes the result up instead.

This is where a model starts to become useful. If one shared set of assumptions can predict these differences across different tasks, it's doing much more than inventing a separate story for each result.

There's a related idea I want to mention, because it matters a lot for managers. It's called constructed preference.

The idea is that things like a default option, an opening question, or a comparison product can help form a preference, rather than just reveal it. Remember the shopper from our first lecture?

She might only decide how much battery life matters once she's comparing phones. A detection model treats her answer as evidence of a preference she already had.

A construction model says the comparison itself helped create it. Classical models can also allow preferences to change, so the useful question is which explanation correctly predicts what happens when we change the process.

Let's go back to the judgment guide for a moment, because now we can see its weakness more clearly. A list like this has no natural place to stop.

Each new pattern gets a name and a suggested fix after it shows up. And these local explanations rarely help predict each other.

They don't tell you when an effect should vanish, or where it's likely to show up next. A theory can do those things.

It explains several observations with fewer ideas, it makes predictions in advance, and it rules out results that would otherwise be possible. Simplicity is nice, but it only really counts if it gives the theory a clear chance to be proven wrong.

So what's the shared idea that quantum cognition uses to connect these patterns? It's called incompatibility, and it's easiest to understand by starting with its opposite.

In the quantum model, compatible judgments leave the same overall answer pattern whichever order you use. That is a prediction of compatibility.

One small observed order effect, on its own, would not establish it. Now picture a manager evaluating a proposal for its opportunity and its risk.

If she thinks about opportunity first, her mind is on growth by the time she gets to risk. If she starts with risk, her mind is on possible losses by the time she gets to opportunity.

If those two paths lead to different patterns of answers, the judgments may be incompatible. In the arrow picture, answering the first question moves the arrow before the second question is even asked.

That's something we can test, and then we can compare different models of why it happens.

This diagram puts the pieces together. At the top is one shared model, and below it are the three patterns we've already met.

The first is that question order can change the answer pattern. The second is that a direct decision can differ from the weighted average of decisions made after an assessment, which is interference.

The third is that judgments of combined ideas, like the pet fish, may need a model that captures how the parts take on meaning together, which is non-separability. Quantum cognition has tools for each of these, but each one comes with extra assumptions, and finding one pattern doesn't guarantee you'll find the other two.

The connection proves its value when the model predicts well across different tasks, including tasks held back while the model was being fitted, so it can't simply have memorized them.

Now, I don't want to give you the impression that the classical model is always wrong. The framework also tells you when the simpler model is perfectly fine.

Suppose a bank checks an account's balance and whether its owner meets a fixed age rule. Checking the balance first doesn't change the age rule.

Reverse the checks and you get the same pair of answers. Because neither check affects the other, one simple table can describe the answers in either order.

The quantum model treats these as compatible judgments, and it gives exactly the same probabilities as the classical calculation. For a task like this, the simpler model is all you need.

Let me finish by connecting this to tools you may already use. Expected utility is a way of comparing options by combining their possible outcomes, how likely each one is, and how much each outcome is worth to you.

Bayesian updating is a way of revising your probabilities as new evidence comes in. Both are still useful, as long as their assumptions fit the decision.

As a manager, a broader model like quantum cognition deserves your attention when it improves predictions about things you care about, like surveys, interfaces, or team decisions. It should explain when context matters, carry useful insight over to a new task, and tell you what result would prove it wrong.

In the next lecture, we'll look at exactly how to hold it to that standard.
