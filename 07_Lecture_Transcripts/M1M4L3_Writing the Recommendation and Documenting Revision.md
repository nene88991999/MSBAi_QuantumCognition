# M1M4L3_Writing the Recommendation and Documenting Revision

In the last lecture, we built recommendations with five parts. Now let's talk about putting them in writing.

A client report can have every statistic right and still lead someone to a decision the evidence doesn't support. Often, the problem is in the nouns, meaning who or what the report says it's about.

The analysis measured a language model, but the conclusion talks about residents. The audit tested one pair of frames, but the recommendation talks about community engagement in general.

So keep each claim tied to what you actually tested: the model or people, the exact setup, the questions, the outcome, and the evidence tier. That way, the reader can see where the result applies, and which bigger claims still need evidence.

Being precise doesn't weaken the report. It makes it more useful, because it separates what the client can do now from what still needs more work.

A good place to set that scope is right at the start, with the decision. Open the report by stating the management decision.

Say what the client has to choose, why it matters, and when it has to be decided. For example, you might open like this: before launching a resident survey, the engagement team has to choose whether to standardize, rotate, or disclose the order of the economic and environmental frames.

From there, the report explains how the evidence affects that choice. And when you introduce each statistic, connect it to the part of the decision it helps with.

Once the decision is clear, explain how the results bear on it. Present them as a pattern, not as separate facts.

Start with the main effect, in percentage points, with its interval. Then give q, and compare its interval with the tolerance you planned.

Then report the interference estimate and the separate bracket comparison. Say which decision rules each result met.

A label like “statistically significant,” on its own, throws away useful information. A sixteen-point difference might matter a lot for the decision, even if the cause is still uncertain.

And a very small difference might pass the QQ check, while giving little reason to change policy. So keep three things separate: how big the effect is, how precisely you measured it, and what it says about the cause.

What those numbers mean depends on who, or what, gave the answers. So state that limit in plain language.

A Tier 2 audit supports a claim about the specific model, version, prompt, persona grid, and questioning process you used. It can test your questions and your analysis, and help you prepare a study with residents.

But its results describe the model's responses under that setup. A Tier 3 audit supports a claim about the people you sampled, answering the survey you actually ran.

Even then, be careful about extending it to other groups, stakes, or settings. Put this limit right next to the result, where the reader is forming an interpretation.

A caveat at the end of the report rarely changes a decision that the executive summary has already shaped.

Once you've stated the result and its scope, build the recommendation around a simple link: because we saw this pattern, change this part of the process. Name the owner, and name the model or group the evidence describes.

Add the review condition and the next study. When the evidence supports a specific action, say it directly.

For example: revise the uneven wording, then repeat the two-order test. The client should know what changes, which outcome you'll be watching, and which result would trigger another look.

And make sure the action doesn't reach further than the evidence does.

All of this comes together in the executive summary, which is the short overview at the top of the report that many readers will read most closely. Organize it around the decision and the evidence behind it.

Start with what the client has to choose. Then describe the audit design and the evidence tier.

Report the results together, and explain which causes are still possible, including the strongest alternative. End with the action, the owner, and the review condition.

Use as many sentences as you need to make those connections clear. The body of the report can then develop the same argument in more detail, with the important qualifications placed right beside the claims they limit.

There's one more thing a strong report needs: a history of how your thinking changed. A record of revisions shows how your judgment developed.

Your judgment becomes visible when a reader can compare what the analyst expected with what the evidence actually supported. So before collecting any responses, write down the direction you predict, the mechanism you think is at work, the classical rival, and what it all means for the decision.

Then your later interpretation can be compared with those first expectations. If a polished conclusion leaves out the original forecast, every result looks like you saw it coming.

A revision log keeps the real order of your reasoning. It shows where the prediction held up, where it changed, what caused the change, and how the recommendation moved as a result.

Every revision needs a trigger, meaning the specific thing that changed your mind. State the exact observation, or the discovery about your design, that changed the conclusion.

For example, an order effect that persists while q clearly falls outside its tolerance challenges the geometric explanation. A small, precise order effect might mean there's less need for a redesign.

And if you discover unequal persona groups or uneven prompts, you need to fix the comparison before choosing a cause at all. Naming the trigger keeps each revision tied to evidence.

It stops a vague feeling of “that's surprising” from taking the place of analysis.

Different observations change different parts of a conclusion, so it helps to separate three kinds of revision. A size revision changes how big you think the effect is, but keeps the explanation.

For example, you predicted twenty points and observed twelve. That calls for a better estimate.

An explanation revision changes the explanation itself, because a firm prediction failed. For example, you predicted q near zero and found a steady departure.

That challenges the model. A scope revision keeps the result for the setting you tested, but narrows who or what it applies to.

For example, describing a Tier 2 result as model behavior narrows the claim, without weakening the pattern you measured. These revisions carry different weight, so say which part changed, and why.

Let's wrap up. A useful account of a revised judgment connects your original expectation to the evidence that changed it.

Explain why the first view seemed reasonable, point to the result or design discovery that mattered, and show how the interpretation, scope, or recommendation changed. Then think about what carries over to your next decision.

It might be a review condition, a direct test of the strongest rival, or a clearer evidence boundary. Each one can improve your next analysis.
