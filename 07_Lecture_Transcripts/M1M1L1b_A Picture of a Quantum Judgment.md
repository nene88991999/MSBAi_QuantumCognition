# M1M1L1b_A Picture of a Quantum Judgment

In the last video, I mentioned that quantum probability is one way to describe judgments that form as you answer. Over the next few lectures we're going to test a quantum model, and it's hard to test something you've never actually seen.

So this short video gives you a picture of what the model says. Don't worry, you won't need any physics.

The model borrows some math from quantum theory to describe how people answer questions. It doesn't claim that the brain is a quantum computer, and we'll come back to that point later in the module.

Let's start with one person and one yes-or-no question. Picture that person's current state of mind about the question as an arrow of length one.

Now give the question two lines at a right angle to each other: one line for yes and one for no. Imagine a light shining straight down onto the yes line.

The arrow casts a shadow on it. The longer the shadow, the more likely the person is to say yes.

So if the arrow points almost along the yes line, it casts a long shadow, and yes is very likely. If it leans toward no, the shadow is short, and yes is unlikely.

In Module 2 you'll see the exact rule, which involves squaring the length of the shadow. For now, just remember that a longer shadow means a more likely yes.

Now for the most important step, which is what happens when the person answers. In this picture, answering moves the arrow.

If she says yes, her arrow swings around, and her arrow now lies along the yes line. That's her new state of mind, and it's where she starts from when the next question comes along.

So answering isn't just reading off a value that was sitting there. It changes the state that the next answer comes from.

If that reminds you of the construction idea from the last video, you're on the right track.

So far we've only had one question. Let's add a second one, with its own yes and no lines.

Sometimes the second question's lines point the same way as the first one's. That's the simplest picture of compatibility.

More generally, think of a bank checking two facts: an account balance and a fixed age rule. Each check leaves the other fact unchanged.

One table can describe all the answer pairs in either order. We call questions like that compatible.

The two facts can be different; sharing one pair of lines is just the simplest drawing. For compatible questions, the quantum model gives exactly the same predictions as ordinary probability.

In other words, the classical model fits inside the quantum one as a special case.

Other times, though, the second question sits at an angle to the first. A manager rating a proposal first for opportunity and then for risk might be an example.

When she answers the first question, her arrow snaps onto one of its lines. From that new position, her shadow on the second question's lines is different from what it would have been.

If you ask the questions the other way around, the arrow snaps somewhere else first. So the order can change the probabilities of the answers.

Questions with these different directions are called incompatible. In this model they make order effects possible, although the size of the effect also depends on the starting arrow.

There's one more idea I want to give you, because you'll hear the word interference a lot. Imagine two groups of people.

The first group answers question A and then question B. The second group skips A and goes straight to B.

Everyone in the first group has to pass through A-yes or A-no on the way to B. Take a weighted average of those two routes, using the share who answered yes and the share who answered no.

To use that average to predict the group that skips A, we assume the response rates along the routes carry over unchanged. That's our fixed-rate classical benchmark.

But the second group never answered A, so their arrow never snapped. Its probability for B, calculated from the squared shadow length, can come out above or below that average.

That difference, between the direct result and the average of the routes, is called interference. In the next lecture, you'll see an example where it pushes a result outside the range a classical model allows.

Let's recap the three ideas I'd like you to take into the next lectures. First, a state of mind is an arrow, and squaring its shadow lengths gives the chances of each answer.

Second, answering moves the arrow, which is why order can matter. And third, skipping a question leaves the arrow where it was, which is why a direct answer can differ from the average of the routes.

Keep in mind that none of this proves the quantum model is right about any particular decision. It's a candidate that makes specific predictions, and those predictions can fail.

Testing them against simpler explanations is what the rest of this module is all about.
