# Agency in AI Assisted Decisions

The preceding lectures connected response histories, procedural authority, and the value of outcomes. We will now apply those ideas to an AI adviser. Imagine a budgeting assistant helping a user review subscriptions. The user wants to reduce monthly spending while keeping services needed for work. The assistant can ask questions, summarize options, recommend cancellations, and perhaps take action. Each capability can affect how the user reaches a decision. Before judging the assistant, we need to specify which capabilities the user authorized and what goal the assistant is meant to serve.

Begin with the decision and the permission. Permission to summarize a subscription is different from permission to select a sequence of reminders. Both differ from permission to cancel or renew automatically. The user may want the assistant to identify overlooked benefits while reserving the final action. Recording the scope makes it possible to evaluate whether the system stays within it. It also helps the user revise a particular permission without abandoning useful assistance altogether. For example, the user could keep a spending summary while disabling retention reminders. The available controls should correspond to these actual choices about the procedure.

Next identify the goal behind the recommendation. The user may value lower spending, reliable access to a work tool, or fewer interruptions. The service providing the assistant may benefit from subscription renewal or continued engagement. Those interests can align in some cases and diverge in others. A system that asks about every benefit of a subscription before mentioning its cost may shift the final judgment. We should examine whether that sequence was chosen to support the user's stated purpose and whether the user can request another order or proceed directly. Calling a system an adviser does not resolve whose interests its procedure serves.

The distinction between a state and a person also matters here. A model can represent an AI system's response process with states and update rules. Those objects describe behavior under the model. They do not, by themselves, establish consciousness, ownership of reasons, or human free will in the system. When we use language-model responses as a synthetic panel, we are studying a particular model and prompting procedure. An order effect in that panel can help evaluate the machine's behavior. Claims about human judgment require human evidence. This is the same boundary introduced in the earlier modules, now applied to the vocabulary of agency.

Suppose the assistant recommends renewal and provides a persuasive explanation. The explanation is another output to evaluate. We can check whether it uses the user's stated goals, accurately describes the offer, and identifies the permission under which it acted. We can also compare the result with the same decision under a reversed sequence. Fluent wording alone cannot show that the user endorses the recommendation or that the system faithfully followed the authorized process. A useful record preserves the questions, answers, permissions, recommendation, and final action. The record allows a reviewer to connect the explanation to what actually happened.

Human control is most informative when it can be exercised at the relevant point. A final confirmation screen may appear after the system has already renewed a subscription. In that case, the timing of confirmation fails to provide the authority its label suggests. A review before the binding action permits a different form of control. An effective ability to stop, revise the goal, or choose a direct route can also matter. These controls need to work under realistic conditions, including when the user is uncertain or changes their mind. Evaluation should follow the whole path from initial permission to final action.

Consider a hypothetical assistant that first asks which subscriptions are essential for work. It then offers to review either costs or benefits first, explains how to skip the review, and asks permission before making a cancellation. The user can change the goal or withdraw action permission. This design makes several decisions visible, but it still needs evaluation. Users may misunderstand a term, overlook a cost, or find the process burdensome. We would examine comprehension, successful refusal and revision, the resulting choices, and the effort required. The purpose is to test whether the procedure provides the control it promises while helping the user pursue the stated goal.

Strategic settings add other people and incentives. A manager may authorize an AI system to conduct a review before a business decision. A rival's expected response may then affect which review the manager chooses. Two procedures can produce the same average action or payoff while forming judgments differently. My work on strategic agency studies such distinctions under explicit assumptions. For our purposes, the practical question is straightforward: who can choose the review, what interests shape that choice, and who can revise it? We should identify those decision makers before interpreting the outcome probabilities as evidence of authority.

A useful evaluation therefore combines behavioral tests with records of permission and control. Estimate whether sequence changes the outcome. Compare plausible mechanisms using predictions that can fail. Connect welfare claims to values supported independently of the response being explained. Examine whether the user can understand, refuse, and revise the procedure before the action becomes binding. Then state what evidence would change the recommendation. These steps bring the course's model comparisons into a concrete managerial setting. They also preserve a central insight from the philosophy: the meaning of a choice depends on the reasons and procedures through which the person can guide it.
