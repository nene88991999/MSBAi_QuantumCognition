# Three Axioms for Choice

In the last lecture, we examined how to choose a criterion and test whether it captures the concern behind an analysis. We will now apply that approach to three requirements from my research on free will in choice. Our example remains the notification setting. Two screens offer off and on. One emphasizes focus, and the other emphasizes connection. We can ask either question alone, or ask one and then the other. A system of choice probabilities records the chances of the answers under all of those procedures. The requirements apply to that whole system. This matters because a single answer, or even a single percentage, provides much less information than the direct and sequenced responses together.

I will begin with the requirement about reasons because it is the most familiar. In the paper it is called A3, reasons-responsive control. Imagine that, for the decision under consideration, protecting study time makes off more valuable to the person than on. A3 asks whether one value ranking fits the response probabilities in every question asked alone. A higher-valued option must be at least as likely as a lower-valued option, and the probabilities must strictly distinguish values somewhere. In our two-option example, off must be at least as likely as on on each standalone screen, with a strict difference on at least one. We keep the label A3 so that the lecture matches the paper. Beginning with it makes the purpose of the comparison easier to see.

The phrase one value ranking carries weight here. We cannot change which outcome is better every time a screen produces an inconvenient result. The same ranking must explain the direct probabilities across the settings being compared. Even then, the axiom establishes consistency with some ranking. To say that the ranking represents the person's own reasons, we need independent support, such as information about the person's goals and circumstances. And A3 is defined for questions asked alone. A change in a second answer after another question does not, by itself, show that A3 fails. Later we will use that distinction to study how an intervention can weaken alignment at a final decision while the original standalone requirement continues to hold.

To understand the other requirements, we need a benchmark for stable preferences. A random-utility model allows different rankings across people or repetitions. At the start of a question sequence, it draws one ranking and holds that ranking fixed for the sequence. Each answer selects the highest-ranked available option. For example, some people may rank off above on, while others rank on above off. A mixture of those people can produce an off share of eighty percent even though each person follows a fixed ranking. The benchmark therefore includes variation across people. It is much broader than a claim that everybody always gives the same answer. When we test it, we ask whether any distribution of such held rankings can reproduce the full pattern.

A1 is called irreducible alternativism. The first part requires openness: at least one direct question gives more than one answer a positive probability. A system that always produces just one answer to every direct question fails that part. The second part rules out any distribution of rankings held fixed within each sequence that reproduces the complete choice pattern. The word irreducible refers to that restriction. An eighty-twenty split by itself establishes neither part of the philosophical interpretation. It can arise from a simple mixture of stable types. A1 asks whether the entire pattern, across the relevant questions and sequences, resists the ranking representation. It is a demanding requirement. Other accounts of agency allow a person to act freely while reliably selecting the best option.

Now broaden the benchmark. Imagine a fixed answer sheet with a response written down for every question. A person might have off recorded for the focus screen and on recorded for the connection screen. The sheet need not come from one consistent ranking. Different people or repetitions can receive different sheets, so we allow a distribution of them. Asking a question reveals the corresponding entry and leaves every entry unchanged. The paper calls this a non-invasive classical representation. Non-invasive means that the questioning does not alter the response variables. Classical here refers to ordinary probability applied to those fixed variables. This is the precise benchmark. Ordinary probability can also describe processes in which memory or evaluation changes, and those processes are outside this fixed-sheet class.

A2, constructive sourcehood, rules out a distribution of fixed answer sheets that reproduces all the direct and sequenced probabilities. Think about the connection question. If its entry stays fixed, asking the focus question first cannot change the connection answer that will be read from the sheet. Across comparable populations, the connection response rate should therefore agree with its standalone rate. A reliable discrepancy challenges that non-invasive representation. The name sourcehood expresses a philosophical interpretation of the requirement. An imposed question can also disturb evaluation, so satisfying the mathematical restriction does not establish that the person owns or controls the process. We will return to that issue when we discuss who chooses the questions.

The two benchmarks have a useful relationship. Every held ranking produces an answer sheet: write down its highest-ranked option for each question. Some answer sheets cannot be generated by a single ranking, so the answer-sheet benchmark is broader. If an open system rules out that broader class, it also rules out the held-ranking class. This gives the implication from A2 and openness to A1. The openness condition remains part of the statement. The reverse implication does not generally hold. A pattern can resist held rankings while still allowing fixed answer sheets. A3 adds a different concern, the relationship between direct probabilities and values. It does not follow simply from openness or from a disturbance caused by questioning.

Consider someone who always selects the highest-valued option. That rule can satisfy A3. It fails A1 because the direct response is never open, and it admits a fixed answer sheet, so it fails A2. The example shows how the proposed requirements classify a system. It also gives a reason to examine the philosophical commitments. A compatibilist account might see reliable, reasoned choice as an exercise of agency. My paper uses a more demanding interpretation for the particular separation it studies. In the next lecture, we will work through a quantum example that satisfies all three requirements. We will also build a classical process with memory that reproduces its responses. Comparing the two will help us see exactly what the axioms establish.
