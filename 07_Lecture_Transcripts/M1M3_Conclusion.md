# M1M3_Conclusion

That's the end of Module 3. Let's pull together what we learned.

The main idea was that an interface becomes part of the process that produces an answer. Asking someone for an assessment can change what they do next.

An opening screen can draw attention to one particular goal. And a synthetic panel's answers depend on which questions, and which earlier answers, the model can see.

Because of that, the original records really matter. Keep the persona descriptions, the questions, the model settings, and the complete conversation.

Check how each response was coded, and which answers went into each calculation. A good record lets you tell the difference between a simple coding mistake and a real change in how the answers were produced.

We also saw that our three statistics each need their own comparison. The main effect compares a decision across the two question orders.

The QQ test uses each person's pair of framing answers. Interference needs a direct group, plus the assessed groups that give us the weighted prediction.

And the bracket asks a separate question: could any mix of those assessed rates explain the direct result? If you plan for each of these from the start, you'll have the data you need.

Finally, always keep the evidence tier next to the claim. A model audit tells you about the model and setup you tested.

A claim about people needs evidence from people. In Module 4, our final module, we'll use that distinction to decide which action the results support, and what evidence would make us change our minds.
