# When a Judgment Settles and Reopens

In the previous module, we examined direct and sequenced choices and asked what those patterns tell us about agency. We will now add a third question to the sequence. This lets us study whether a judgment remains settled after a person considers something else. Return to the notification example. You choose off to protect study time. If the app asks the same question again immediately, you may repeat that answer. If it first asks about staying connected, you may answer the focus question differently when it returns. Both patterns concern persistence, meaning whether a response continues through time. Understanding them requires following the person's sequence, rather than looking only at the final group percentage.

Let A stand for the focus question and B for the connection question. These letters simply keep the sequence easy to read. In an A followed by A sequence, the same question is asked twice with no other question in between. We compare the first and second answers. If they match, we observe immediate repetition on that occasion. Across many occasions, we can estimate how often they match. Repetition can be consistent with a settled judgment, but it can also reflect memory, habit, or a desire to appear consistent. The observation identifies stability of the response under that procedure. Explaining why it is stable requires more information.

Now consider A followed by B followed by A. The first and last questions use the same wording and offer the same alternatives. B sits between them. We compare the person's first and last answers to A. A change records return revision: the earlier answer differs when the question returns. That is a description of the history. It might occur because considering connection changes the evaluation of focus. It could also reflect learning, distraction, pressure, or an error in reporting. If a new urgent message arrives between questions, the available information has changed. A clean comparison therefore states what happened between responses and which features were intended to stay fixed.

There is a reason to retain the individual histories. Imagine one hundred illustrative respondents. Fifty initially choose off and fifty choose on. At the final A question, fifty again choose off and fifty choose on. The overall off share is unchanged. Now inspect the paths. Twenty people moved from off to on, and another twenty moved from on to off. Forty people changed their answer. The unchanged total combines two offsetting movements. Alternatively, the same initial and final totals could arise if everyone repeated the first answer. These two cases have identical endpoint shares and very different persistence. A study that saves only the totals cannot distinguish them.

The relevant measures answer different questions. The net change in the off share subtracts the initial percentage from the final percentage. In the illustration, it is zero percentage points. The revision rate counts people whose first and last answers differ and divides by the number with valid paired answers. Here it is forty percent. When histories are incomplete, the denominator needs to be stated and the missing records examined. If the people who stop responding are especially likely to revise, a complete-case rate can misrepresent the intended population. Preserving identifiers and question order makes these checks possible. It also prevents a response from one person being paired with a response from another.

The projective quantum model supplies one account of how persistence and reopening can occur. After an answer to A, the state settles along the direction for that response. With the same question asked immediately and no other evolution or reporting error, the model predicts the same answer. Asking an incompatible B question can change the state before A returns. A different final answer then becomes possible. This is a conditional prediction of a specified response process. If people often change on immediate repetition, that simple process needs revision or may be unsuitable. The assumption of no intervening change is part of the prediction, and should be visible when we test it.

Longer histories can also help compare models that agree on short sequences. In the previous module, a memory process reproduced the direct and two-question percentages of our quantum example. Additional sequences provide more observations against which to compare its rules. A flexible memory process may still fit, so a longer record does not automatically select a unique explanation. The useful question is which pre-specified predictions distinguish the candidate processes. We should also avoid importing a numerical bound from one model into another. Restrictions linking influence and revision depend on assumptions about the state, questions, and updating process. Their validity comes from those assumptions and the evidence supporting them.

How does this relate to agency? A person may want a decision to remain settled so they can get on with studying. The same person may value an opportunity to reconsider when a relevant reason arises. Persistence and revisability can therefore both matter. A repeated answer could reflect endorsement, meaning that the person continues to accept the decision as their own. It could also reflect a barrier to changing it. A revised answer could be deliberate reconsideration or an unwanted disturbance. Evidence about permission, understanding, reasons, and effective revision helps interpret the response history. Neither stability nor switching is sufficient on its own to establish the person's control.

For a manager reviewing an interface, the practical implication is to preserve both the choice history and the surrounding conditions. Record which questions were asked, which answers became binding, and whether the user could pause, refuse, or return. Examine aggregate rates alongside changes within valid paired histories. Then connect the pattern to the mechanism the evidence supports. We have moved from asking whether order matters to asking how a decision persists through a sequence. The next lecture takes up a further issue: who has the authority to choose that sequence in the first place?
