# A Quantum Model of Constructive Choice

In the last lecture, we introduced three requirements for a system of choices. We now have enough background to examine a complete example. Return to the app's focus and connection screens. Each offers the same alternatives, notifications off or on. For this example, off has a higher value for the chooser. We hold that ranking fixed. The numbers I will show are constructed from a model. They are not survey results or measured effects of an actual app. Their purpose is to demonstrate how direct and sequenced choices can fit the requirements. Once we understand the construction, we can ask what a study would have to establish before applying it to real people.

Here are the direct choices. When the focus question is asked alone, the model gives about an eighty-five point four percent chance of off. When the connection question is asked alone, it gives the same chance of off. The remaining fourteen point six percent goes to on in each case. Both questions therefore favor the higher-valued option when asked alone. That establishes A3 for the value ranking in this example, including the required strict distinction. The direct questions are also open because both alternatives have positive probability. Notice what we have held constant: the available options and their values. The two questions provide different ways of considering the same decision.

The quantum model represents the initial evaluation with the unit arrow we introduced earlier in the course. Each question has directions corresponding to off and on. Squared shadow lengths give the answer probabilities. For this construction, the initial arrow sits halfway between the two off directions. The two sets of directions are forty-five degrees apart. That geometry gives the same direct off probability for either question. You do not need to calculate an angle to follow the comparison. The useful point is that a single starting evaluation can favor off in both ways of asking. The model specifies the state and the question directions before calculating the probabilities. It does not choose a new starting state to fit each number separately.

Now ask the focus question before the connection question. In the projective version of the model, answering the first question settles the state along the direction of that answer. Projective is the technical name for this update rule. If the first response is off, the state lies along the focus-off direction. If the first response is on, it lies along the other focus direction. From either of those states in this particular construction, the next connection response has a fifty percent chance of off. The two routes differ in how often they are reached, but their conditional connection probabilities are the same. Averaging across the routes therefore gives fifty percent off at the connection screen.

Compare the connection question with itself under the two procedures. Asked alone, its off probability is about eighty-five point four percent. Asked after focus, its off probability is fifty percent. The difference is about thirty-five point four percentage points. Percentage points describe the subtraction of the two percentages. This is a change in the probability of the same answer to the same target question. We compare the full group reaching that target with the direct group. Looking only at the people who first chose off would answer a different, conditional question. Keeping those denominators clear will matter when we move from a constructed model to sampled data.

Why does this result establish A2 in the exact construction? A fixed answer sheet has one connection entry. Reading the focus entry first leaves the connection entry unchanged. Its direct and later response shares therefore agree when the same distribution of sheets applies to both procedures. Our constructed connection shares differ, so no such distribution can reproduce them. A2 holds. Because the direct choices are open, A1 follows as well. A3 also holds because both standalone questions favor off. The reduction in the later off probability does not remove that standalone property. It shows that answering an earlier question can change the final probability even when the direct questions share the stated value ranking.

Now consider a competing process built with ordinary probability and memory. At the first question, it draws off with an eighty-five point four percent chance. It stores the question and answer. If the same question is immediately repeated, it repeats the stored answer. If the question changes, it draws off or on with equal probability and updates its memory. That process reproduces the direct and two-question probabilities in our example. It also gives immediate repetition of an answer. Its response variables change with the questioning, so it lies outside the non-invasive benchmark that A2 excludes. This comparison identifies the scope of the result. The quantum model provides one construction, while a classical process with state changes can supply another.

To compare those processes empirically, we would need predictions on observations they handle differently. Longer question histories, independent measures of memory, or a carefully justified restriction on the response process might help. Merely obtaining a good fit to the three displayed percentages would leave both accounts available. A model comparison must use the same observations and make its flexibility visible. If one account is allowed to invent a separate rule for every sequence, fitting past data becomes easier without guaranteeing useful new predictions. Earlier modules introduced that distinction between fitting and testing. The philosophical setting gives us another reason to keep it in view.

A study also needs comparable groups and a stable target question. If one group receives new information about an urgent message, the difference can reflect information rather than the modeled update alone. If respondents anticipate a later question and alter their first answer, the preparation conditions have changed. With finite samples, estimated percentages vary, so uncertainty accompanies the comparison. And if the responses come from a language model, the result describes that model under the tested procedure. Evidence about people requires people. We can now see what the example contributes: it connects stated requirements to a complete response process and a numerical prediction. The next lecture asks what additional evidence connects such a pattern to a person's agency.
