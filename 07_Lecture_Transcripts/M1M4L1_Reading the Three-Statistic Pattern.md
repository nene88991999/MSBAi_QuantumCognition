# M1M4L1_Reading the Three-Statistic Pattern

Welcome to Module 4, our final module. At the end of Module 3, we worked through an illustrative audit with three statistics.

Now we need to figure out what to do with them. Imagine a client who has to decide whether to change the way it asks questions.

The audit gives you three results, and each one answers a different question. The main order effect tells you how much the client's outcome changes when you swap the order of the questions.

The QQ statistic, q, compares how often people agree with themselves across the two framing questions in each order. And interference compares a direct action with the weighted prediction we built from assessed judgments.

Alongside interference, there's the bracket check, which asks whether any mix of the assessed rates could produce the direct rate. In this lecture, we'll learn to read these three results together.

That matters because one dramatic number can hide a pattern that's actually uncertain or inconsistent.

Let's start with an example. Go back to the proposed data center from Module 3.

Suppose that when residents hear about the economy first, support is 68 percent. When they hear about the environment first, support is 52 percent.

That's a gap of sixteen percentage points. Now suppose the organization has a rule: it moves forward only if support reaches 60 percent.

One order lands above that line and the other lands below it. In other words, the order of the questions alone could change the decision the client records.

That's a real management concern. But notice what the gap doesn't tell us: it doesn't tell us why it happened.

Tired respondents, uneven wording, learning from the first question, and incompatible judgments could each produce a gap like this. We need to tell these explanations apart before we pick a fix.

So the support gap shows us the consequence. To learn something about the cause, we turn to the second statistic, q.

Remember that q uses each respondent's answers to the two framing questions, not the final support question. In each order, you add up the share who said yes to both and the share who said no to both.

That's the agreement rate. Then you compare the agreement rates between the two orders.

The geometric model from Module 1 predicts that the difference should be zero. In practice, you compare the interval around your estimate with the tolerance you set in advance.

Here's the key point: a large outcome effect can sit right alongside a q close to zero. If the entire q interval is within tolerance, that supports a small departure from one prediction.

It still doesn't explain why the outcome moved. On the other hand, a clear, precise departure from zero challenges the model, as long as the design checks pass.

So the main effect and q give you two different pieces of evidence.

The third statistic comes from a different kind of comparison. Instead of swapping question order, we change whether people make an assessment before they act.

Think back to Meridian Pay in Module 3. Interference compares the direct rate with the weighted prediction.

And the two action rates after each assessment, one after a fraud judgment and one after a legitimate judgment, set the edges of the classical bracket. Any mix of those two rates has to land between them.

So we ask: where does the direct rate fall? If it's clearly outside the bracket, we can rule out every explanation that keeps those two assessed rates fixed.

If it's inside, some mix could produce it, and comparing it with the observed weights becomes a separate test. Both the size of the gap and its bootstrap interval matter.

In your report, include the direct rate, the two ends of the bracket, the weighted prediction, the interference estimate, and the uncertainty around each comparison.

Now let's put all three together. Here's one pattern that tells you a lot.

The main effect is big enough to matter, the q interval stays within tolerance, and the direct rate clearly falls outside the bracket. Each part does a different job.

The main effect shows the client what's at stake. The q result passes one firm prediction of the model.

And the bracket result rules out any explanation that only reweights fixed response rates. Even so, there's still a rival to consider: a two-stage classical model, where the assessment changes the decision rule that comes after it.

You'd want to test that rival directly. And remember the scope of what you've shown.

Your conclusion is about the response process under the conditions you audited, and the models you actually tested.

Here's something that surprises many people: a bigger effect can actually give weaker support for the model you're proposing. Compare two results.

The first is a sixteen-point effect, but its q interval sits completely outside the tolerance. The second is a six-point effect, with its q interval completely inside the tolerance.

The first result is the bigger business problem, but it challenges the geometric model's prediction. The second passes that check, although we'd still need the bracket comparison and the rival tests.

And if either q interval is wide, we can't say much either way. That's why we write down these possible branches before we analyze anything.

Effect size tells you how much the decision moved. The pattern tests tell you which explanations are still standing.

Now, a small effect isn't a failure. In fact, a small, precisely measured effect can be very useful for a decision.

Suppose the main effect is small, the intervals are narrow, and the other checks all stay within their classical ranges. Then the simple, fixed-judgment model survives the audit, and the client can keep the simpler process with more confidence.

Just be clear about what you're claiming. The conclusion applies to the group or model you tested, the question pair, the order, and the outcome.

A good way to say it is: this audit found no meaningful departure, at the level of precision it achieved.

Whatever the pattern, you'll have competing explanations to deal with. The best way to test one is to ask what else it predicts, and then go look.

If fatigue might explain the result, check how long people took and whether they skipped more of the later questions. If the explanation might be wording asymmetry, meaning one question is worded more vividly than the other, rewrite them in parallel form and have someone independent review them.

If people might be learning something from the first question, ask what they learned and whether that knowledge predicts the direction of the effect. If people might be guessing what the study wants, ask them what they think it's about.

Keep a table with each rival, what it predicts, and what you found. Also note any part of the pattern the rival can't explain, so readers can judge the comparison for themselves.

Once you've tested the rivals, you can write your verdict. A good verdict connects five pieces: what's at stake for the client, the statistical pattern, the strongest rival, the evidence tier, and how far the conclusion reaches.

Here's an illustrative Tier 2 example. You might report a sixteen-point effect in the language model you tested, a q interval within tolerance, and a clear bracket violation.

Next, you'd report what the prompt review and the rival test found. Then conclude that the questioning process changed the responses in this model setup, and say which explanations are still possible.

Every part of that statement should trace back to a result from your analysis or a documented check of your design.

Let's wrap up. Think of the three results as one connected argument.

The main effect shows what's at stake for the decision. The q statistic checks the agreement pattern.

And the direct-versus-assessed comparison tests the weighted prediction and the bracket. Together with your rival tests, they support a conclusion about the process and the model or people you actually studied.

Some findings will point toward keeping a simpler process. Others will point toward a redesign or more evidence.
