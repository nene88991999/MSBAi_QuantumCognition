# M1M2_Conclusion

We've reached the end of Module 2, so let's look at how far we've come. We started with a vague worry, that question order might change people's answers, and turned it into something we can actually investigate.

The first step was separating the order of items from the order of questions about one item. That distinction tells you what to vary in an audit, and which part of the process a fix would need to change.

We also learned to keep two results separate. The main effect tells you how much the decision itself changed between the two orders.

The QQ test looks at something different: how often each person's two framing answers agree, compared across the orders. It checks one specific prediction of the geometric model.

If the result is precise and within the tolerance you set in advance, that prediction stays in the running, but so do the competing explanations.

We also saw that uncertainty matters for both results. The bootstrap resamples whole response records, many times over, to show how much a statistic could vary by chance.

Then you compare the resulting interval with a threshold you chose before seeing the data. A small, precise effect and a wide, uncertain one look different, and they call for different decisions.

Put together, a useful audit connects four things: the business question, the comparison, the full response records, and the rules for what you'll do with each result. And it tests rivals, such as learning or uneven wording.

In Module 3, we'll bring AI into the picture. We'll apply this approach to AI-assisted decisions and synthetic panels, where the conversation with the AI becomes part of the process we're studying.
