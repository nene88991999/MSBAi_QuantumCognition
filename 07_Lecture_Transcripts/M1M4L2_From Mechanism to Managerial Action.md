# M1M4L2_From Mechanism to Managerial Action

In the last lecture, we learned to read the three statistics together and write a careful verdict. Now comes the part your client really cares about: what should they do?

Suppose the evidence shows that question order changes support. Telling the client to “improve the survey” is too vague to act on.

The right action depends on the explanation that the evidence supports best. If the problem is uneven wording, the fix is to revise the questions.

If people are learning from earlier information, the fix is a deliberate order for that information. If the frames are truly incompatible, then question order becomes a design choice that someone has to own.

And if the audit is uncertain, you need more evidence. So a good recommendation explains how the diagnosis connects to the change.

When the cause is still unclear, suggest a test the client can easily undo, and say what it will tell you.

Let's go through the possible findings one at a time, starting with the simplest. Sometimes the audit shows that the different procedures give answers that are practically the same.

When the evidence supports a stable, fixed judgment, keep the simpler process. One question order might be cheaper to run, or easier to interpret.

The client can choose whichever order is least burdensome, as long as the tested options lead to the same decisions within a tolerance agreed on in advance. Keeping a process that passes these checks saves the cost of an unnecessary redesign.

But keep an eye on it. Check whether response patterns change over time, differ across important groups, or start to matter more for the business.

A second kind of finding points to the questions themselves. Suppose q falls far from zero, and a review of the wording shows that one frame describes consequences in vivid detail, while the other uses abstract language.

In that case, you need to fix the instrument, which is the survey or questioning process itself. Rewrite the questions so they're parallel.

Give them the same amount of information and the same answer options. Run a few short interviews to check how people understand them, and then repeat the two-order audit.

Keep the old version and its results, too. That record explains why you made the change, and it stops anyone from quietly trying wordings until they get the answer they want.

Until the repaired questions can separate a real order effect from uneven measurement, treat your recommendation as provisional.

A third kind of finding looks different. Sometimes respondents learn something useful from the first question.

In that case, rewording won't help, because the cause is still there. We call this information carryover, and it calls for a deliberate order of questions.

Here's an example from a customer journey. An early product review might give a customer real information that reasonably changes how much a later offer is worth to them.

Or, in a risk workshop, one scenario might reveal a dependency that the next question relies on. When knowledge checks and timing data support this explanation, design the order as a learning sequence.

Put the background information first, keep persuasion separate from measurement, give people enough time to think, and report the final judgment as one that was informed by the sequence. That way, you keep the useful learning, while reducing accidental differences between teams or interfaces.

The decision changes again when the questions help create different perspectives. When the evidence points to incompatible frames, someone has to own the question order.

Choose an owner, explain why certain considerations come first, and test reasonable alternatives. One common tool here is counterbalancing, which means assigning comparable groups to each order.

It shows you how sensitive the answers are to the order. But remember that averaging the two groups still mixes two different procedures.

In a public process, you might tell people what order they'll see, present both frames, and let them revise their answers. In a private workflow, you might compare orders on accuracy, fairness, and the cost of errors.

Either way, test whether the chosen procedure serves those goals. A model of construction can describe what's happening, but it can't decide the organization's priorities for it.

So far we've talked about question order. But a procedure can also change by adding an assessment step before an action, like the Meridian Pay example from Module 3.

That audit leads to a concrete test of the workflow. If an explicit assessment changes what reviewers do, compare both versions on the costs that matter.

In fraud review, that might mean false blocks, missed fraud, the time each decision takes, how much reviewers trust the AI, and how good their overrides are. The construction account tells you that the assessment helps produce the action.

It doesn't tell you which outcome matters most; that's management's call. A staged pilot, where some reviewers use the Direct console and others use Assess-first, lets you compare the full set of outcomes.

Just decide how you'll weigh the different errors before you pick a winner.

Whatever action you recommend, the evidence tier sets how far it can reach. Remember the three tiers from Module 3.

A Tier 2 result, from a model audit, can support revising prompts, improving a synthetic-panel process, training a team, or planning a study with people. It can also support a small internal pilot that's easy to undo, when the decision is about how a model behaves.

But a policy that affects people needs Tier 3 evidence from the right population, in the right setting. So in your recommendation, name the model or group you tested, the action you're proposing now, and the next step for gathering evidence.

That lets the client act on what the evidence supports today, while planning for a bigger decision later.

Of course, new evidence or rising costs can change whether any of these actions still makes sense. That's why every recommendation needs a review condition.

A review condition is a result, decided in advance, that would make the organization stop, reverse, or revise what it's doing.

For example, a frame-order policy might be reconsidered if a Tier 3 study shows an effect with its entire interval between minus five and plus five percentage points, or if q fails after the questions are repaired, or if the chosen order widens the gap between groups beyond an agreed limit.

A redesigned review screen might be stopped if accuracy drops, or if reviewers start relying on the AI too much. These conditions give the decision owner a clear reason to revisit the action if its benefits, or the explanation behind it, don't hold up.

Let's put it all together. A complete recommendation has five parts: an action, an owner, a mechanism, an evidence tier, and a review condition.

You don't need a long paragraph for this; a few short sentences will do. Here's an example.

The community engagement lead should revise the synthetic survey so it discloses both frame orders. That's the owner and the action.

The Tier 2 audit shows that question order changes the tested model's responses. That's the evidence tier and the mechanism.

Run the same comparison with residents before treating that pattern as evidence about people. And reconsider the proposed frame-order policy if the human effect's entire interval falls inside the range of minus five to plus five percentage points chosen for this decision.

That's the review condition.

Let's recap. The audit result shapes the management response.

A stable result supports keeping the simpler process. A flawed instrument calls for repair.

Information carryover calls for a deliberate learning sequence. Incompatible frames call for clear ownership of their order.

And effects at a human-AI handoff call for a comparison based on stated business costs. The evidence tier sets how big the action can be, and a review condition protects the client as new evidence comes in.

A good recommendation makes this whole chain easy to follow in plain language. In the next lecture, we'll look at how to write it up for the client.
