# Designing a Concept of Agency

In the last lecture, we distinguished the recorded choice from the capacities and procedures that might make it the person's own. We now need to decide how to use the word agency in a particular analysis. Suppose a product team says its app gives users agency because every screen has two buttons. The statement is easy to check. Count the buttons. But imagine that selecting skip returns the user to the same reminder, while selecting continue opens the setting. Two buttons are present, yet refusal has little effect. The difficulty begins with the criterion we chose. A criterion is a requirement used to decide whether something qualifies. If the purpose is to evaluate meaningful control, button count leaves out a consequential part of the process.

Conceptual engineering studies how to assess and improve concepts for a purpose. Herman Cappelen introduces the subject as an effort to evaluate the concepts and other representational tools we use. Here, the practical task is to develop a useful meaning of agency for evaluating a decision procedure. We can begin with ordinary usage, but we should explain why a proposed criterion serves the question. For the notification app, suppose the purpose is to protect the user's authority over how a setting is chosen. That purpose makes understanding, effective refusal, and revision relevant. A different purpose, such as predicting which button will be clicked, may require a different set of variables. Stating the purpose first gives other people something substantive to evaluate.

Now propose a criterion. We might say that users retain procedural authority when they understand the offered interaction, can proceed directly, and can revise the resulting setting. Procedural authority means control over how the decision is reached. The criterion is more informative than counting buttons because it describes what the controls must allow. It also leaves questions open. What degree of understanding is enough? How much effort makes a bypass ineffective? Could a person choose to delegate the sequence to an adviser? Those questions help refine the proposal. A definition becomes useful through the distinctions it makes and the reasons supporting them. Giving a definition a familiar name does not remove the need to examine those reasons.

A counterexample is a case that exposes a problem with a proposed claim or criterion. Consider a user who asks the app to show a connection reminder before changing notifications. The reminder is chosen, and permission can be withdrawn. A rule that treats every preliminary question as a loss of agency would classify this helpful delegation as a failure. The case suggests a revision: examine who authorizes the interaction and whether that authorization remains effective. Now consider the opposite case. A skip button appears, but selecting it triggers three more retention questions. A rule based only on the presence of a skip button would miss this obstacle. Testing both cases helps us make the criterion precise without defining every convenient interface as acceptable.

The next step is measurement. A definition states what will count as agency in the analysis. An operational measure specifies how observations will be collected and used. To evaluate effective refusal, we could record the path after a user selects skip, the time required to reach the setting, and whether the choice can be completed. To evaluate understanding, we could ask users to explain what the reminder changes and what declining it permits. Those observations can be informative even when no single score settles the question. We should explain how each observation relates to the criterion. A high number of clicks, for example, could indicate exploration or an obstacle. The path and the user's purpose help interpret it.

There is also a risk in choosing a definition around the features a company already provides. Suppose the service calls agency the ability to select any option that increases engagement. That definition removes the user's study goal from consideration. A careful evaluation asks whose interests the criterion serves and which cases it excludes. The same discipline applies to a theoretical model. An axiom is a requirement stated at the start of an argument. A theorem then shows what follows if the requirements and other assumptions hold. The proof establishes that conditional relationship. The choice of requirements remains open to philosophical scrutiny, and their application to an app requires evidence. This separation lets us use formal results while keeping their purpose and interpretation visible.

For our course, the sequence is now clear. Start with the decision and the concern. State a criterion, test it against difficult cases, and identify observations that would show whether it is met. Revise the criterion when it misses a case that matters for the stated purpose. In the next lecture, we will study three axioms from my research. They make specific demands on choice probabilities. We will ask both what those demands imply and what further evidence is needed to connect them to a person's reasons and control. That is how conceptual engineering helps us evaluate a formal account of agency.
