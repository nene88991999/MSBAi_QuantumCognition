# Agency and Consumer Welfare

In the last lecture, we examined who chooses the questions and whether users can refuse or revise a procedure. Today we will calculate how a sequence can affect the value of the final outcome. Consider a subscription service. A consumer can renew or decline. Before that decision, the service may ask a retention question. We will hold the price, offer, available alternatives, and relevant information fixed. This isolates a particular comparison: the effect of the preceding question under the stated model. If the service offers a discount or supplies new information, we need a different calculation because the decision itself has changed.

We also need values for the consumer. Suppose independent evidence indicates that declining is worth ten dollars more than renewing for the consumer being considered. The ten dollars is a value difference for this example. It is not a fee charged by the service, and we do not infer it simply from which button the consumer clicks after a question. Consumer welfare concerns how valuable the outcomes are for that consumer. With the value difference fixed, each renewal represents a ten-dollar loss relative to always declining. A procedure that changes the probability of renewal therefore changes expected loss. Expected means the average implied by the probabilities over comparable repetitions.

Begin with a direct decision, with no preliminary question. Suppose its renewal probability is twenty percent. Multiplying twenty percent by the ten-dollar value difference gives an expected loss of two dollars relative to always declining. Imagine one hundred comparable occasions. The model predicts twenty renewals on average. Each contributes ten dollars of loss under the stated values, giving two hundred dollars in total, or two dollars per occasion. This repeated-occasion interpretation makes the expectation concrete. A particular consumer on a particular occasion either renews or declines. The expected amount summarizes uncertainty before that response is realized.

The projective model in my agency-welfare research gives a range for what one preceding binary question can do. Binary means that the question has two possible answers. Starting from a direct renewal probability of twenty percent, the model permits a later renewal probability between ten and sixty percent. The endpoints require appropriately chosen question directions under the model. An actual retention question may produce a smaller change, and its effect needs to be estimated. For the illustration, we compare three specified procedures: a consumer-favored question giving ten percent renewal, direct choice giving twenty percent, and a firm-favored question giving sixty percent. The service is assumed to benefit from renewal.

Apply the same ten-dollar value difference to all three. At ten percent renewal, expected loss is one dollar. At twenty percent, it is two dollars. At sixty percent, it is six dollars. The comparison with direct choice is six minus two, or four dollars of additional expected loss under the firm-favored question. The comparison with the consumer-favored question is six minus one, or five dollars. These are different comparisons with different baselines. Naming the baseline prevents the same result from being reported as either four or five dollars without explanation. The underlying probabilities and values have stayed the same throughout.

There are several conditions behind those numbers. We have treated the ten-dollar value difference as stable and independently justified. We have also excluded differences in the effort, time, or value of the procedures themselves. If one question takes substantial time, its cost belongs in a fuller welfare calculation. If a discount makes renewal more attractive, the ten-dollar difference must be reconsidered. Different consumers can have different values, so multiplying an overall renewal effect by one average value may be misleading when effects and values vary together. A population analysis should state whose values are being used and how the comparison combines them.

A3 helps explain another distinction. It evaluates the relation between values and the questions asked alone. An earlier question can change a later response without changing whether the original standalone probabilities satisfy A3. In the current example, the direct decision favors declining, the more valuable outcome. A preceding intervention can nevertheless raise the chance of renewal. The welfare calculation concerns that final probability and the independently stated values. It therefore adds information about the consequences of the sequence. It does not require relabeling the original standalone requirement every time a later response moves.

Procedural authority remains a separate part of the evaluation. Two services could have the same renewal probability while differing in whether the consumer requested the preceding interaction. The outcome-based expected loss would match under the same values. The person's authority to select or refuse the interaction could still differ. A person may value that authority in its own right. Quantifying such a procedural value would require additional evidence or assumptions; the one-, two-, and six-dollar figures include only the stated outcome difference. Keeping this scope explicit lets an organization discuss both the monetary comparison and the allocation of control.

For a recommendation, bring together three records. The response study estimates what the procedure changes. Valuation evidence supports the consequence for the consumer. The interface and permission record describes who can choose, refuse, or revise the process. If valuation is missing, an organization can still report a response difference and examine control, while identifying the information needed for a monetary claim. In the next lecture, we will apply this reasoning to an AI adviser. The adviser may help a user consider relevant reasons, and it may also change the sequence in pursuit of another goal. Its permissions and evaluation should make that distinction visible.
