# M1M4L4_Building and Defending the Ten-Minute Client Story

Welcome to our final lecture. Last time we discussed how to write a report.

Now imagine the client gives you ten minutes to present your analysis, with a discussion afterward. How would you use that time?

Here's one structure that works well, with six slides: the decision, the competing explanations, the audit design, the combined result, the evidence boundary, and the recommendation. You can adapt it to your audience and the time you have.

Each slide answers one question, and what you say out loud connects the answers and adds the qualifications. Your audience needs enough time to understand why the numbers matter and how they change the decision.

So choose the evidence that supports your argument, and practice with the slides you'll actually show.

Let's walk through the six slides. Slide one states the decision and what's at stake.

Name the client owner, the choice they face, and the cost of getting it wrong. For our data center example, the engagement lead might need to choose how to order the economic and environmental frames before a resident survey.

If nobody manages that order, the support estimates could be unstable, and the process could lose credibility with the community. Keep this opening concrete.

Your audience should understand the real problem before they ever hear the words “quantum cognition.” A short description of the choice, and a picture of the process, are usually enough to set the scene.

Once the audience understands the decision, slide two lays out the competing explanations, along with the predictions that tell them apart. The fixed-judgment model expects the same pattern of answers in both orders.

A construction explanation allows the first judgment to change the answer to the second. The geometric model adds a specific prediction, the QQ test.

Then include the strongest classical explanation for change, such as memory or learning, and say what extra evidence it would predict. Show which result would challenge each explanation, and when the evidence might leave several of them still standing.

Those competing predictions are the reason you need a careful design. Notice how each slide sets up a question that the next slide answers.

Slide three shows the audit design: the frame pair, the two orders, the model or group you tested, the sample size, and the three statistics. For a Tier 2 audit, name the model version, the prompt setup, the persona grid, and the date the answers were generated, so the audience can judge the comparison.

Slide four shows the combined result. Lead with the main effect, then put q and interference beside it, each with a short explanation and its interval.

Say which decision rule the results meet. Your audience should be able to repeat the logic in one sentence: the order changed the outcome, q was near or far from zero, and the direct rate stayed inside or went outside the classical range.

The results then need a clear scope, and that's slide five: the evidence boundary and the strongest rival. Show the evidence tier clearly.

Say what the result supports and what you haven't measured yet, and report in one line whether the evidence supported the classical rival's prediction. This slide protects your recommendation from overreaching, and it prepares the audience for the first skeptical question.

Slide six gives the recommendation: the action, the owner, the mechanism, the evidence scope, the review condition, and the next test. For example, a Tier 2 construction result might justify revising the survey questions, disclosing both frame orders, and preparing a Tier 3 study with residents before any decision about the population.

Close with the decision the client can make now, and the evidence that will guide the next one.

So how much time does each slide get? This timeline shows one way to split up the ten minutes.

The decision gets one minute. The competing explanations get ninety seconds.

The design gets ninety seconds too. The results get two and a half minutes, because that's where the audience needs the most help.

The evidence boundary gets one minute, and the recommendation gets two minutes. Those six slides add up to nine and a half minutes, which leaves thirty seconds for the transitions between them.

You can adjust these to fit the decision, but make sure you also plan time for questions. Practice out loud, with the actual slides and reveals.

And if it feels rushed, cut some detail rather than talking faster.

Now let's talk about the discussion afterward. Expect questions about what the evidence supports, and about what could change your recommendation.

A good answer starts with the conclusion. Then it gives the relevant design choice and one or two pieces of evidence.

State the limit of what you can claim, and pause so they can follow up. If a question exposes a gap, say so, and explain how you'd test it.

It helps to prepare for questions about the frames, the strongest rival, the evidence tier, and the proposed action. The goal isn't to win every question.

It's to show that your recommendation rests on evidence that someone else can check.

One question you'll almost certainly hear is: does this apply to residents? That's a question about scope, so answer it with the evidence tier.

Say whether the analysis tested an estimator, a language model, or the actual people involved. If it's Tier 2, name the model version, the prompt setup, the persona grid, and the date.

And be clear that similar headline percentages, or similar pattern checks, don't prove that the model and people share the same cause. Then say what your tier does support.

That might be testing your questions and analysis, revising the survey, a model-level action you can easily undo, or a claim about people. Finish with the next study you'd need to make a claim about the people who matter for this decision.

The client will also want to know when your advice would change. So prepare for this challenge: what result would change your verdict?

Give specific thresholds or patterns. For example, if repaired questions still gave q consistently far from zero, that would weaken the standard geometric model we're testing.

If the direct rate returned inside the classical range, you'd drop the claim of a bracket violation. Its difference from the weighted prediction would still need its own comparison.

If a Tier 3 sample showed a narrow interval around a tiny order effect, that would weaken the case for a policy change. And if a pilot raised error costs, you'd reverse the recommendation.

Specific review conditions like these show that the framework limits what you believe and what you do. They also give the client a plan for monitoring.

Let's close the course with the big picture. Many decision systems treat a judgment as a stable quantity, just sitting there waiting to be measured.

A sound audit asks a deeper question: is the questioning process reading that judgment, or helping to form it? It looks for order effects, interference, and wholes that fixed parts can't explain.

It tests clear predictions against strong classical alternatives, treats the human-AI workflow as part of the measurement, and matches each claim to its evidence tier.

You can use the same approach for any decision: identify the judgment you're studying, compare believable explanations, run a test that could prove your favorite explanation wrong, and act within the limits of your evidence. Thank you for joining me.
