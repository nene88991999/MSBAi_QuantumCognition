# Evidence about Agency

We have just worked through a system that satisfies the three proposed choice requirements. We also saw that a classical process with memory can reproduce its displayed responses. Today I want to connect that lesson to a claim an organization might make. Suppose a report says that question order changed users' choices, and therefore the users demonstrated free will. The first part describes a comparison. The second adds an interpretation about the people making the choices. To evaluate the report, we need to identify the steps connecting them. Doing so helps an organization reach a useful conclusion without asking one percentage to carry more meaning than the study provides.

Begin with the observed comparison. What response is measured, among whom, and under which procedures? For example, we might compare the share choosing off when the connection question is asked alone with the share choosing off after the focus question. If the groups differ in their reasons for using the app, the raw difference can combine procedure and population differences. Random assignment of comparable participants to procedures can help separate those sources. The study should keep the wording, available alternatives, and relevant information consistent with the intended test. These conditions define the result we are trying to estimate. They also determine which explanation a difference would challenge.

Next ask how precise the estimate is. Imagine an illustrative difference of six percentage points with an interval from one to eleven. The interval describes sampling uncertainty under the analysis used. A second study might estimate the same six-point difference with an interval from negative four to sixteen. The central estimates match, while the range of values consistent with the analyses differs. We should examine the interval alongside a practical tolerance chosen for the decision. A result whose interval includes zero has not established that the effect is negligible. A practical claim about a small effect needs evidence that the relevant range lies within the chosen tolerance. This is the same discipline we used in the earlier audit modules.

The next step concerns the representation. A credible discrepancy between direct and sequenced responses can reject fixed response variables under comparable preparation conditions. It leaves processes with memory, learning, or state disturbance available. A quantum model becomes more informative when its additional predictions survive tests against those rivals. For example, a model may restrict paired responses or patterns in longer histories. We should state those restrictions before inspecting the new results. A result consistent with a restriction is evidence to weigh with the alternatives. The QQ equality, which concerns aggregate probabilities of paired agreement across reversed orders, illustrates a restriction that needs its own assumptions and uncertainty analysis.

Now consider the person's reasons. A3 asks whether direct probabilities agree with some common value ranking. Suppose a researcher chooses the ranking only after seeing the responses. The resulting consistency may be mathematically valid, while its interpretation as the person's own reasons remains unresolved. Independent evidence about goals helps. In the notification example, that could include a stated plan to protect study time and information about circumstances in which an urgent message matters. Reasons can change legitimately when circumstances change. The evaluation should identify the decision and timing to which a ranking applies. A study that fixes values for a comparison should explain why that is reasonable in the setting.

Control over the procedure adds another source of evidence. Could the person refuse the preliminary question? Did the skip option actually reach the setting? Could the person revise the final answer before it became binding? Did they understand what the interface was asking them to authorize? These observations address causal and procedural control. A company-imposed sequence can generate an order effect even when the person has little authority over it. The same mathematical pattern can therefore appear in procedures with different relationships to agency. We should record who chooses the sequence and which answer determines the action, alongside the response frequencies.

There is also a difference between an explanation offered by a system and evidence that a person endorses a decision. An AI adviser might generate a fluent account of why notifications remained on. That text could be useful to inspect, but it does not establish the user's reasons. A user's explanation matters too, while its timing and the surrounding conditions affect its interpretation. We can compare the decision with a previously stated goal, offer a genuine opportunity to revise it, and ask about understanding. These sources can support one another or reveal a disagreement. Keeping them separate in the record makes the disagreement informative.

A careful recommendation brings those pieces together. It identifies the response difference, the representation challenged, and the evidence about reasons and authority. For example, a service could preserve direct access to the setting while offering a reminder that users can request or refuse. That recommendation follows a stated concern for procedural control. Its effects on comprehension, effort, and final choices still need evaluation. The question of agency has now led us to an actionable design issue. In the next module, we will examine how decisions remain settled or become open again over time, and how control over that process affects both the person and the organization.
