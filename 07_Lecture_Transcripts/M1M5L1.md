# What Makes a Choice Your Own

In the previous modules, we saw that the order of questions can change a recorded choice. Today I want to ask what that means for the person making it. Imagine that you have set aside an hour to study. You open an app to turn notifications off. Before showing the setting, the app asks whether you want to stay connected with friends. You answer yes. When the setting appears, you leave notifications on. The service records a choice, but we still have a question. Did you reconsider a goal, forget what you came to do, or follow a path the service designed for its own benefit? Each explanation gives the same click a different meaning. This is where the philosophical discussion becomes useful. It helps us say which question about the choice we are trying to answer.

Start with agency. In ordinary language, an agent can act in pursuit of purposes and respond to reasons. A reason is something that counts in favor of an action. An uninterrupted hour supports turning notifications off. Waiting for an urgent message could support leaving them on. A person's decision can depend on how those reasons fit the situation. Control concerns whether the person can guide what happens. If the app ignores your selection, having a clear purpose does little to determine the setting. Free will asks what kind of freedom and control make an action attributable to you. That question includes disagreements about alternatives, responsibility, and how an action arises. We will use agency as the broader starting point and explain particular claims about free will when they become relevant.

The notification case also helps us introduce ontology. Ontology asks what exists and what kind of thing it is. For example, is agency a capacity of a person, a property of an individual action, or a relationship between the person and the surrounding institutions? Those possibilities direct attention to different evidence. A capacity is something a person can exercise, even when they do not exercise it on a particular occasion. You may be able to reconsider your notification setting while choosing to leave it as it is. A record of one click describes an action. It does not automatically describe the full capacity behind that action. Asking which object we mean helps prevent a claim about one observation from becoming a claim about the whole person.

We can now separate several questions about the same screen. An ontological question asks what kind of capacity agency is. An empirical question asks what people actually do when the screen changes. An epistemological question asks what evidence would justify our belief that they controlled the decision. Epistemology is the study of knowledge and its justification. A normative question asks what should be protected, such as a person's ability to refuse the reminder. These questions can overlap. Evidence about whether a skip button works matters to a recommendation that people should be able to skip. Still, a measured change in clicks cannot, on its own, supply the principle behind that recommendation. Keeping the question visible helps us connect the evidence to the claim it can support.

A similar distinction matters when we use a mathematical model. Earlier, we represented an evaluation with an arrow. The arrow is an object in the model. It lets us calculate probabilities and describe how one answer affects the next. Interpreting that arrow as a feature of the world requires an argument about what it represents. In quantum cognition, the arrow can represent a person's current evaluation without making a claim about physical quantum processes in the brain. Think of a map of a transit system. Its lines help describe routes, even though the actual tracks do not have the exact shapes printed on the map. A model's usefulness and its interpretation are related questions. Accurate predictions help us evaluate it, while the interpretation tells us what those predictions are about.

Free will brings one more distinction into view: determinism and predictability. Determinism means that a complete prior state of the world, together with its laws, fixes what follows. Predictability concerns what an observer can work out with the information available. A process could be deterministic and still be difficult to predict. Philosophers called compatibilists hold that free will can be compatible with determinism. Many accounts in that family emphasize how an action relates to the person's reasons and control. Incompatibilists hold that free will and determinism cannot both hold. Within that group, libertarian accounts defend free will and reject determinism. Other incompatibilists reject the relevant freedom. These names identify positions in a debate. They are not labels for good and bad decision makers.

Consider two ways to set notifications. In one, a random number generator selects off on half the occasions. In the other, you consider your plans and reliably choose off during study time. The random procedure is unpredictable, but it has no study goal of its own. Your reliable response can express a purpose even when someone predicts it correctly. This comparison shows why the frequency of an outcome is an incomplete account of agency. We also care about how the response connects to reasons and whether the person can guide the procedure. Christian List's work asks how capacities and available alternatives enter decision theory, including settings with predictable choices. My axiomatic approach imposes more demanding restrictions on observed choice patterns. We will state those restrictions explicitly, so you can evaluate what the approach captures.

Return to the person trying to study. We can record the final setting, whether the reminder was requested, whether skipping worked, and what reasons the person gives for the decision. Each observation answers part of the question. A changed setting may reflect reconsideration. A working bypass gives evidence about procedural control. A reason given afterward still needs to be assessed in context. We now have a vocabulary for discussing these differences without assuming one click settles the philosophical debate. In the next lecture, we will use that vocabulary to examine how a definition of agency should be chosen, and how a seemingly convenient definition can miss the very concern we wanted to study.
