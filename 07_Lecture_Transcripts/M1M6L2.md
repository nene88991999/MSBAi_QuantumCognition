# Who Controls the Questions

The last lecture showed how an earlier judgment can persist or reopen after another question. We now need to ask who decides whether that intervening question is asked. Imagine two people trying to turn notifications off. The first has asked the app to remind her about urgent contacts before changing the setting. She can disable that reminder whenever she wishes. The second receives the same reminder without requesting it, and the app blocks direct access to the setting. Both eventually leave notifications on. Their final responses match, while their authority over the procedure differs. A record containing only the final setting would miss that difference.

Procedural authority is control over how a decision is reached. It includes who selects the preliminary questions, whether the person can proceed directly, and which answer determines the action. My research on agency and welfare calls the right to choose a preceding interaction, or go directly to the decision, a sequencing right. The term identifies a particular form of authority. It does not promise that every chosen sequence will produce the person's preferred outcome. Someone can authorize a reminder and still make a mistake. Authority concerns who can choose the process, while the quality and value of the resulting decision require additional evaluation.

To make the distinction concrete, follow the path through the app. The user first sees an offer of a reminder. If the reminder is optional, selecting skip should reach the notification setting. If the user selects it, the connection question appears before the setting. Afterward, the user should understand whether the response has merely opened the next screen or actually changed notifications. A button labeled continue can conceal that distinction. The relevant control is the one that determines the action. To assess the procedure, we need to observe where each option leads and when the choice becomes binding. Labels alone provide an incomplete description of those paths.

Delegation adds an important case. Delegation means allowing another person or system to act within an agreed scope. You might ask an adviser to remind you of consequences you tend to overlook. That can be a deliberate way to pursue your own purpose. Informed and revocable delegation can preserve procedural authority. Informed means the person understands the relevant permission. Revocable means the person has an effective way to withdraw it. A person could authorize a reminder while withholding permission to change the setting automatically. Treating those permissions separately helps the system respect the scope of the delegation.

A practical evaluation can test whether refusal works. Suppose the app displays a skip button. Pressing it returns to the same screen, or adds another mandatory question. The visible label suggests an option that the implemented path does not provide. Another interface reaches the setting, but only after a long delay that makes refusal costly. The importance of that delay depends on the context and the user's circumstances. We should record the actual effort, time, and outcome rather than infer control from a screenshot alone. An accessibility barrier can matter for the same reason: an option may exist in the design while being difficult for a particular user to exercise.

Understanding also needs to be examined in context. A long permission statement can list every consequence and still leave users uncertain about what will happen. A useful test asks a person to explain, in ordinary language, what the system may do and how to stop it. Observed actions can then show whether the available controls behave as expected. The point is to connect permission to the actual decision procedure. A remembered click on an agreement box supplies some evidence about an interaction. It leaves open whether the person understood the scope and retained an effective way to revise it.

Organizations also have purposes. A service may choose a connection reminder because it expects more notifications to remain on. That could support users who want frequent contact, while conflicting with users trying to protect study time. The analysis needs to identify the goal and the affected person. A higher engagement rate measures a business outcome. Its relationship to the user's welfare depends on the user's values and the circumstances. The allocation of procedural authority adds another concern even when outcome rates happen to match. Keeping these objects distinct makes it possible to assess both the business result and the user's control.

Consider a redesign that offers three clearly described paths: proceed directly, review a reminder now, or authorize the same reminder for future changes. The paths should lead where their descriptions promise, and future permission should be easy to revise. This is a hypothetical design for teaching, not an observed product. Its purpose is to make the allocation of authority visible. Evaluation would examine users' understanding, whether refusal and revision work, and the consequences for choices and effort. It would also compare outcomes for users with different goals. A redesign needs evidence about its operation and effects before an organization can judge whether it improves the process.

We can now connect procedural authority to the earlier axioms. An imposed question can change evaluation and generate the pattern associated with A2. Direct probabilities can also fit a value ranking under A3. Those properties leave open whether the person authorized the sequence or can refuse it. The agency claim therefore needs evidence about the relationship between the person and the process. In the next lecture, we will hold the offer and the person's outcome values fixed and calculate how a change in sequence affects expected welfare. That calculation will complement the question of who controls the procedure.
