# Introduction to Module 7

The earlier modules gave us ways to study how a question can shape a judgment. We also considered what a choice reveals about a person’s reasons and control. We now bring these ideas together by asking how a result becomes a defensible recommendation. A defensible recommendation is one whose evidence and reasoning can withstand careful questions.

Imagine that a company finds more favorable responses when it asks about convenience before cost. Before changing its survey, the company needs to understand that difference. First, reconstruct the result. Check which responses were counted, how the comparison was calculated, and how much uncertainty remains. Next, examine the explanation. The sequence may have changed the judgment, but differences in wording or context could also matter. Then connect the finding to an action. A revised survey might be worth testing, with a clear condition for deciding whether it improves the process.

This sequence gives peer critique a practical purpose. Another reader can identify a missing comparison, a hidden assumption, or a conclusion that goes beyond the evidence. We can then revise the argument and explain the reason for the change. A useful analysis may support a clear action. It may also show exactly which uncertainty must be resolved before the company acts. Both outcomes help a manager make a better decision.
