Begin with the [module notes](../04_Module_Notes/README.md), then use the introductory selections below. The collection includes contrasting explanations and technical extensions. Optional readings do not add assessment requirements; Canvas identifies any assigned selections. Publisher links may require university library access. Author manuscripts and open-access versions are linked where available.

## Judgment and Constructed Preference

**Bettman, James R., Mary Frances Luce, and John W. Payne (1998). “Constructive Consumer Choice Processes.” Journal of Consumer Research, 25(3), 187–217.** [Publisher](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/25/3/187/1795625). Introductory conceptual reading. Focus on how the task and the decision strategy shape choice. Identify a process explanation that could be compared with a quantum probability model.

**Schwarz, Norbert (1999). “Self-Reports: How the Questions Shape the Answers.” American Psychologist, 54(2), 93–105.** [University-hosted paper](https://cci.drexel.edu/faculty/sgasson/Readings/Schwarz%20%5B1999%5D%20Self-reports%20-%20How%20the%20questions%20shape%20the%20answers.pdf). Introductory application. Follow the examples of wording, response format, and context. Use them to critique the measurement procedure in a proposed company survey.

**Hogarth, Robin M., and Hillel J. Einhorn (1992). “Order Effects in Belief Updating: The Belief-Adjustment Model.” Cognitive Psychology, 24(1), 1–55.** [Publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/001002859290002J). Optional model comparison. Read the description of the updating process before the formal details. It supplies a substantive alternative to treating every order effect as uniquely quantum.

## Quantum Probability and Model Tests

**Pothos, Emmanuel M., and Jerome R. Busemeyer (2013). “Can Quantum Probability Provide a New Direction for Cognitive Modeling?” Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 36(3), 255–274.** [Author manuscript](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/2428/1/Can%20quantum%20probability%20provide%20a%20new%20direction%20for%20cognitive%20modeling.pdf). Conceptual overview. Read the motivating examples and keep track of the assumptions behind each model. The associated commentary offers further perspectives.

**Wang, Zheng, and Jerome R. Busemeyer (2013). “A Quantum Question Order Model Supported by Empirical Tests of an A Priori and Precise Prediction.” Topics in Cognitive Science, 5(4), 689–710.** [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12040). Optional foundation for Module 2. Follow how the model produces a quantitative restriction before looking at the empirical comparison.

**Wang, Zheng, Tyler Solloway, Richard M. Shiffrin, and Jerome R. Busemeyer (2014). “Context Effects Produced by Question Orders Reveal Quantum Nature of Human Judgments.” PNAS, 111(26), 9431–9436.** [Open-access article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4084470/) · [Author PDF](https://jbusemey.pages.iu.edu/quantum/PNAS2014.pdf). Core empirical example. Inspect the answer-pair tables and the QQ restriction. Separate the authors' broader interpretation from what agreement with that restriction alone establishes.

**Yearsley, James M., and Jerome R. Busemeyer (2016). “Quantum Cognition and Decision Theories: A Tutorial.” Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 74, 99–116.** [University repository](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/18714/) · [Author PDF](https://jbusemey.pages.iu.edu/quantum/YearselyBusemeyerJMP.pdf). Optional technical companion. Work alongside the probability and order notebooks. Focus first on states, projections, and sequential probabilities.

**Boyer-Kassem, Thomas, Sébastien Duchêne, and Eric Guerci (2016). “Testing Quantum-Like Models of Judgment for Question Order Effect.” Mathematical Social Sciences, 80, 33–46.** [Author PDF](https://www.sebastien-duchene.fr/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Testing-quantum-like-models-of-judgment-for-question-order-effect.pdf). Optional critical reading. Examine additional restrictions and tests of particular model classes. A model comparison should state which version is being evaluated.

**Dzhafarov, Ehtibar N., and Maria Kon (2018). “On Universality of Classical Probability with Contextually Labeled Random Variables.” Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 85, 17–24.** [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07847). Optional conceptual extension. The preprint was first posted in 2017. Focus on why context labels matter and why failure of a fixed-context model does not invalidate classical probability generally.

**Busemeyer, Jerome R., Riccardo Franco, and Emmanuel M. Pothos (2009). “Quantum Probability Explanations for Probability Judgment ‘Errors’.”** [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/0909.2789). Optional extension on probability judgments. This citation identifies the linked preprint, whose author list and title differ from the later journal article. Compare the proposed mechanism with the alternatives described by the authors.

## Human-AI Decisions and Synthetic Respondents

**Humr, Scott, and Mustafa Canan (2024). “Intermediate Judgments and Trust in Artificial Intelligence-Supported Decision-Making.” Entropy, 26(6), 500.** [Open-access article](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/26/6/500). Core application for Module 3. Distinguish the intermediate judgment, the subsequent decision, and measured trust. Consider what would be needed to transfer the finding to a different workplace.

**Carroll, Micah D., Anca Dragan, Stuart Russell, and Dylan Hadfield-Menell (2022). “Estimating and Penalizing Induced Preference Shifts in Recommender Systems.” ICML, PMLR 162, 2686–2708.** [Paper and PDF](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/carroll22a.html). Optional application. Focus on the decision to evaluate and constrain a recommender's influence on preferences. Read the modeling assumptions before interpreting a proposed welfare measure.

**Argyle, Lisa P., Ethan C. Busby, Nancy Fulda, Joshua R. Gubler, Christopher Rytting, and David Wingate (2023). “Out of One, Many: Using Language Models to Simulate Human Samples.” Political Analysis, 31(3), 337–351.** [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1017/PAN.2023.2) · [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06899). Optional empirical reading. Identify the model, conditioning information, and human benchmark. Ask which aspects of a response distribution are being validated.

**Bisbee, James, Joshua D. Clinton, Cassy Dorff, Brenton Kenkel, and Jennifer M. Larson (2024). “Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models.” Political Analysis, 32(4), 401–416.** [Publisher and open-access paper](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5). Core comparison with Argyle and colleagues. Examine the distinction between matching averages and supporting statistical inference. Connect that distinction to the project's Tier 2 boundary.

**Aher, Gati V., Rosa I. Arriaga, and Adam Tauman Kalai (2023). “Using Large Language Models to Simulate Multiple Humans and Replicate Human Subject Studies.” ICML, PMLR 202, 337–371.** [Paper and PDF](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/aher23a.html). Optional validation study. Compare performance across tasks rather than treating a successful replication as a universal guarantee.

## Uncertainty and Reproducible Computation

**Lakens, Daniël (2017). “Equivalence Tests: A Practical Primer for t Tests, Correlations, and Meta-Analyses.” Social Psychological and Personality Science, 8(4), 355–362.** [Open-access article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502906/). Conceptual companion for Modules 2 and 4. Focus on specifying a meaningful range in advance. The course's 95% interval-versus-tolerance rule is a teaching decision rule; the article explains formal equivalence tests and their own interval conventions.

**NIST/SEMATECH, “Bootstrap Plot.” Dataplot Reference Manual.** [Official guidance](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/refman1/auxillar/bootplot.htm). Technical reference for resampling. The project uses whole persona blocks because its six conditions are linked by persona. Identify the independent sampling unit before choosing what to resample.

**NumPy, “The Absolute Basics for Beginners.”** [Official tutorial](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/user/absolute_beginners.html). Start here for arrays, indexing, and simple calculations. The course examples use NumPy on an ordinary computer.

**Python Software Foundation, “The Python Tutorial.”** [Official tutorial](https://docs.python.org/3/tutorial/). Reference for students who have programmed before and are new to Python. Sections on lists, functions, dictionaries, and files are particularly useful for the audit records.

**Project Jupyter, “Try Jupyter.”** [Official notebook demos](https://jupyter.org/try). Optional introduction to running cells in a notebook. Use the course notebooks for the actual examples and record outputs in order.

## Nathan Yang's Book and the Course

The four [module notes](../04_Module_Notes/README.md) adapt relevant material from *Quantum Cognition for Management*, including the decision default, order effects, the Meridian and Juno cases, and the Python lab. Each note lists its chapter connections. Cases in these notes are fictional, with explicitly illustrative numbers. The runnable examples state their imposed inputs.

The broader manuscript also covers subjective time, organizational attention, strategic interaction, and formal results. Those topics are extensions beyond this four-module course. The course companions provide a focused route through the material; the reading list above supplies the primary sources needed to compare explanations.
